\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Quantization lets large language models run on cheaper hardware, but a smaller
model is not automatically a better one: it can lose accuracy, run slower in some
runtimes, or save disk without saving the memory that actually limits inference.
We measure these trade-offs for one 7B-class instruction model,
Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct. Against a bf16 baseline we compare 8-bit and 4-bit
post-training quantization (LLM.int8(), NF4, and official GPTQ and AWQ) on
capability benchmarks (MMLU, GSM8K, HumanEval, IFEval) and a set of efficiency
measurements on a single RTX~4090. The 4-bit GPTQ and AWQ checkpoints give the best
overall trade-off: they are the only variants that cut both disk and GPU memory
while staying close to baseline speed, at a modest quality cost. Two results run
against intuition: LLM.int8() is the slowest variant of all and saves no disk, and
NF4 saves memory but still decodes slower than bf16. Together they show that
quantization is mainly a memory technique rather than a speed one. We also
implement and run four catalogued extensions on the same box: a JudgeBench
meta-evaluation, W8A8/SmoothQuant self-quantization, a quantized KV cache, and
QLoRA recovery. These sharpen the memory-versus-speed picture.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and Research Questions}

Many LLMs are too expensive to serve in full precision, and post-training
quantization is the standard remedy. Several practical methods exist: LLM.int8()
\citep{llmint8} for 8-bit weights with mixed-precision handling of outliers; GPTQ
\citep{gptq} and AWQ \citep{awq}, which use a small calibration set to push weights
to 4 bits; SmoothQuant \citep{smoothquant} for 8-bit weights and activations; the
NF4 data type from QLoRA \citep{qlora} for 4-bit storage in the bitsandbytes
ecosystem; and KV-cache quantization such as KIVI \citep{kivi} for long-context
inference. None of these is automatically beneficial: the same 4-bit model can
be smaller yet slower, or degrade unevenly across capabilities, so the
practical trade-off is what we set out to measure. We organise the study around
four questions from our proposal:

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
\item[\textbf{RQ1}] Which method gives the best quality--memory--speed trade-off
  for a 7B-class instruction LLM?
\item[\textbf{RQ2}] Are some capabilities (reasoning, coding, QA, instruction
  following) more sensitive to quantization than others?
\item[\textbf{RQ3}] Does quantization improve real inference speed, or mainly
  reduce memory?
\item[\textbf{RQ4}] (optional) Can light QLoRA-style adaptation recover quality
  lost after 4-bit quantization?
\end{enumerate}

Our core study is the proposal's minimal successful version (one model, five
variants, four benchmarks, full efficiency profiling), and we then implement the
catalogued extensions as a gated code layer and run the four that fit our GPU
budget.

\section{Experimental Setup}

\paragraph{Model and variants.} All experiments use Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct
\citep{qwen25} at a pinned commit, run on a single on-demand RTX~4090
(24\,GB, sm\_89). Every variant, including the bf16 baseline, runs on the same GPU,
so the speed comparison is apples-to-apples with no cross-hardware caveat.
Table~\ref{tab:variants} lists the five core variants. LLM.int8() and NF4 quantize
the baseline weights at load time, so their disk footprint is the full bf16
checkpoint; GPTQ and AWQ are official pre-quantized 4-bit checkpoints served by the
gptqmodel Marlin kernels through optimum.

\paragraph{Benchmarks and protocol.} Quality is measured with the EleutherAI
lm-evaluation-harness \citep{lmeval}: MMLU \citep{mmlu} (accuracy, aggregated with
a weighting over its 57 subjects), GSM8K \citep{gsm8k} (exact-match under flexible
extraction, since the strict-match number is a formatting artefact for instruct
models), HumanEval \citep{humaneval} (pass@1, with code execution), and IFEval
\citep{ifeval} (prompt-level strict accuracy). Decoding is greedy and
deterministic, with \texttt{do\_sample=False} set at both load and generation.
MMLU and GSM8K use the chat template with 5-shot multi-turn prompting; HumanEval
and IFEval are 0-shot. Efficiency is profiled separately (\S\ref{sec:eff}): the
model is loaded once, then we measure disk size, load and generate peak VRAM,
prefill and decode throughput at short, medium, and long prompts (128, 1024, and
4096 tokens), and the largest batch size before running out of memory.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Quality: capability sensitivity (RQ2)}

Table~\ref{tab:quality} and Figure~\ref{fig:quality} report quality across the four
benchmarks. The main finding is that quantization is remarkably benign for quality
at 7B: every 4-bit variant stays within about 1.5 points of the bf16 baseline on
MMLU and GSM8K. Sensitivity is uneven across capabilities, which answers RQ2.

Knowledge and math are close to lossless: MMLU and GSM8K barely move, and 8-bit is
essentially unchanged. The MMLU shifts are small but, at a $\pm$0.4 standard error,
resolvable; most other deltas are within roughly one standard error and should be
read as trends rather than firm rankings. Coding shows the widest spread, with
bnb-int8 losing about four points on HumanEval, the single largest drop; the 4-bit
methods give up a point or two, though at HumanEval's $\pm$3-point standard error
only the bnb-int8 gap approaches significance. Instruction following degrades only
mildly, within about one standard error, and bnb-nf4 essentially ties the baseline.
The 8-bit variant is the clearest illustration that more bits do not uniformly mean
higher quality: it is near-lossless on knowledge and math yet gives the worst
HumanEval score of any variant.

\subsection{Efficiency: memory vs.\ speed (RQ3)}
\label{sec:eff}

The efficiency profile (Table~\ref{tab:eff}, Figure~\ref{fig:eff}) gives the
clearest and most surprising findings, and answers RQ3 directly: quantization is
primarily a memory technique, not a speed one.

LLM.int8() is the slowest variant. Its decode throughput collapses to about five
times slower than bf16, because of the mixed-precision outlier decomposition, and
it saves no disk since it stores the full bf16 checkpoint, making it the worst of
both worlds on the speed and disk axes. NF4 is the memory-not-speed case: it cuts
generate VRAM sharply and doubles the maximum batch, but still decodes below bf16.
GPTQ and AWQ are the only variants that improve memory without paying much in
speed. They cut disk by about 2.7 times, roughly halve generate VRAM, double the
maximum batch, and hold decode to within about a quarter of baseline (28--29 vs.\
37\,tok/s) --- a modest slowdown next to the 2--5$\times$ of the other quantized
variants. Prefill is unaffected across all variants, because quantization touches
the memory-bound decode phase rather than the compute-bound prefill.

\subsection{The trade-off and a recommendation (RQ1)}

Figure~\ref{fig:tradeoff} places all variants in the quality--speed--memory space,
and the Pareto picture is clear. bf16 buys top speed at the highest memory;
bnb-int8 is dominated, being both slow and disk-heavy; bnb-nf4 trades speed for
memory; and GPTQ and AWQ sit at the sweet spot, with roughly half the memory, the
largest batch, and decode within about 25\% of baseline (a modest slowdown, versus
2--5$\times$ for the other methods) for a small quality cost. Under
limited VRAM we recommend an official GPTQ or AWQ checkpoint. NF4 is
the best fallback when no pre-quantized checkpoint exists, since it needs only the
base weights, and LLM.int8() is hard to recommend on this hardware.

\section{Extensions}

We implemented all five catalogued extensions as a gated, separate code layer
(\texttt{qquant.ext}) that reuses the core harness without touching it, and ran the
four that fit the GPU budget; adding Mistral-7B as a second model was skipped
because it would re-spend the entire compute budget. The whole extension run cost
under \$1 of GPU time.

\paragraph{EXT-2: JudgeBench.} We evaluate each variant as a pairwise judge on
JudgeBench \citep{judgebench}, a set of objective-correctness pairs, scoring
agreement with the gold label under position-bias control, where both the A/B and
B/A orders must agree. Figure~\ref{fig:judge} shows that quantization does not
degrade judging: the 4-bit variants are, if anything, marginally better than bf16.
The absolute numbers are low because Qwen2.5-7B is a weak, heavily position-biased
judge on these hard pairs (only 22 to 33\% of its verdicts are order-consistent), a
property of the base model rather than of quantization.

\paragraph{EXT-3: W8A8 and SmoothQuant.} We self-quantized a W8A8 checkpoint
\citep{smoothquant} (per-channel int8 weights with int8 activations) from the
same C4 calibration set as our 4-bit self-quant recipes, using llm-compressor
\citep{llmcompressor}. The checkpoint is produced and loads natively, but two
things emerged on the HF runtime. First, compressed-tensors decompresses W8A8 to a
bf16-resident tensor (15.2\,GiB in VRAM), so it yields disk-only savings and no
inference-memory benefit. Second, its \texttt{generate()} path is severely
runtime-confounded on sm\_89, running at about 4.5\,s per iteration, so a full
quality sweep is impractical: the slowdown is itself the result. W8A8 is best understood as a vLLM or kernel-path technique, not an
HF-generate one.

\paragraph{EXT-4: Quantized KV cache.} Using the transformers v5 quantized cache
(quanto, int4), we measured peak generation VRAM at a 3072-token context
(Figure~\ref{fig:kvcache}). The int4 KV cache trims the KV-plus-activation
transient by about 22\%, but slows decode by roughly 20 times, because quanto
dequantizes the cache on every step. This is a
clear, quantitative confirmation of the runtime-confounded throughput caveat: a
quantized KV cache trades a modest memory saving for a large (\textasciitilde20$\times$)
speed cost on the HF backend, and is only worthwhile with a fused kernel path such as KIVI
\citep{kivi} or vLLM.

\paragraph{EXT-5: QLoRA recovery (RQ4).} We verified the full QLoRA
\citep{qlora,lora} recovery pipeline end-to-end: a LoRA adapter of rank 16, trained
on the frozen NF4 base over general instruction data and guarded by a fail-fast
check that the training corpus never overlaps the evaluation benchmarks, together
with an adapter-aware load path. Training converged, and the adapted model loads
with the adapter active and generates coherently. A full recovery
measurement (enough training steps to move benchmark accuracy, followed by a
re-evaluation) is a longer campaign we leave to future work; here we contribute
the validated, contamination-safe machinery.

\section{Conclusion}

For a 7B-class instruction model on a single RTX~4090, 4-bit GPTQ and AWQ are the
best default under limited VRAM: they cut disk and generate VRAM by more than half,
double the feasible batch size, and keep decode near baseline, all for a small
quality cost (RQ1). Capability degradation is uneven, with
coding the most sensitive and knowledge and math the least (RQ2). Most importantly,
quantization is a memory technique rather than a speed one (RQ3): LLM.int8() is
about five times slower than bf16 and saves no disk, NF4 saves memory while still
decoding below baseline, and only the calibrated 4-bit kernels of GPTQ and AWQ
deliver memory savings for only a modest (\textasciitilde20--25\%) decode slowdown. The extensions reinforce this: W8A8
and the int4 KV cache both work yet are runtime-confounded on the HF backend, and
quantization does not degrade judging in this small-sample test. The main limitations are single-seed
evaluation and the runtime confound on the self-quant and KV paths, which a
fused-kernel serving stack such as vLLM would resolve; multi-seed runs, the second
model (Mistral-7B \citep{mistral7b}), and a full QLoRA recovery measurement (RQ4)
are the natural next steps.
